# People are aware of the impact of defaults and use them strategically

- URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S1930297526100461/type/journal_article (DOI 10.1017/jdm.2026.10046)
- Authors: Omid Ghasemi, Ali Ghane-Ezabadi, Razieh Hojjat, Mahsa Afshin Mahjoub, Tohid Arastouy Irani, Elahe Gholampour, Moslem Solhirad, Ben R. Newell
- Journal: Judgment and Decision Making, Vol. 21 (2026), e23
- Published online: 2026-09-04
- Type: Peer-reviewed study, 3 experiments (N = 160, 161, 149)

## Key points

- The study tests "default neglect", the idea that people do not understand the power of defaults. The data reject it.
- Participants set defaults for other players in a card game. They chose the default that helped a teammate (or hurt an opponent) in about 85% of eligible trials.
- They expected 62 to 66% of players to stick with whatever default they set.
- Observers who only saw a default inferred which option was better in 79.5% of trials.
- When a teammate set the default, observers read it as a recommendation. When an opponent set it, observers read it as a warning and inverted it.
- Implication: a default is read as advice, and its meaning depends on whether the receiver sees the setter as on their side.
- Ratings of why defaults work: loss aversion 67.8, implicit recommendation 65.2, effort 51.1 (0 to 100 scale).
- Limits: simplified lab game, hypothetical payoffs, students and an Iranian general sample.
- For us: a "741 recommends" default works only while the client sees 741 as a teammate. Any sign of self-interest flips the signal.

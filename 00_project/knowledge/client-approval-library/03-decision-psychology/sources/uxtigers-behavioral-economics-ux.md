- Underlying studies named: Jachimowicz et al. 2019 (default meta-analysis, 58 studies); Johnson & Goldstein 2003 (Science, organ donation); Madrian & Shea 2001 (401k); Scheibehenne et al. 2010 (choice overload meta-analysis, 50 experiments); Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman 2015 (moderators); DellaVigna & Linos 2022 (126 nudge RCTs, 23 million people); Luguri & Strahilevitz 2021 (dark patterns); Brown et al. 2024 (loss aversion meta-analysis); Tversky & Kahneman (framing).

## Key points

- Defaults are the strongest UI lever. A meta-analysis of 58 studies shows an average shift of about 27 percentage points in binary choices.
- Defaults work through three forces: effort (no action needed), endorsement (the preset reads as a recommendation) and endowment (once it is "mine", leaving it feels like a loss).
- Design rules for defaults: pick a defensible default, explain why it was chosen, make changing it as easy as accepting it, and use active choice when preferences really differ.
- Choice overload is real but conditional. A 2010 meta-analysis of 50 experiments found a mean effect near zero. Overload appears when options are complex, effort is high, preferences are unclear, or time is short.
- Comparisons grow fast: 6 options create 15 pairs, 24 options create 276 pairs.
- Guideline: offer 3 to 5 curated options, keep the full set reachable, support side-by-side comparison.
- A recommendation ("Most teams choose Standard") spares users comparisons they cannot make well. It only helps if it truly serves the user and the reason is visible.
- Realistic nudge effect: published studies average 8.7 pp, but complete nudge-unit records average only 1.4 pp. Expect small, steady gains, not miracles.
- Dark patterns work short-term but cause anger. Mild ones doubled acceptance of a dubious service, aggressive ones nearly quadrupled it, and they hit the least-educated users hardest.
- Six ethics tests for any nudge: transparency, symmetry, publicity, self-judgment, regret metrics, vulnerability. Failing one is a red flag.
- Losses weigh about twice as much as equal gains (mean coefficient 1.955 across 607 estimates). Redesigns are judged as losses against the old state, so show the final state next to the change.
- Present bias: a near deadline ("this Friday") recruits procrastination to the design's side.

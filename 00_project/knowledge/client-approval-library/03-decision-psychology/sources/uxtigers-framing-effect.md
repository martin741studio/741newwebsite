# The Framing Effect in UX: Identical Facts, Opposite Decisions

- URL: https://www.uxtigers.com/post/framing
- Author: Jakob Nielsen (UX Tigers)
- Published: 2026-08-06 (page metadata `article:published_time`)
- Type: UX research summary with 12 guidelines
- Underlying studies named: Tversky & Kahneman 1981 (Science, disease problem); McNeil, Pauker, Sox & Tversky 1982 (NEJM, 424 physicians); Levin & Gaeth 1988 ("75% lean" vs "25% fat"); Levin, Schneider & Gaeth 1998 (three framing types); Gigerenzer group 2007 (natural frequencies, 160 gynecologists); Nunes & Drèze 2006 (endowed progress, car-wash cards); European Commission 2022 (dark patterns on 97% of popular sites)

## Key points

- The same fact, said as a gain or as a loss, changes choices. 72% chose the sure option in the gain frame; 78% chose the gamble in the loss frame.
- Doctors were also affected: 18% vs 44% preferred radiation, only because survival vs mortality wording changed.
- Attribute framing: "75% lean" beef was rated better than "25% fat" beef.
- Natural frequencies ("1 in 5") beat percentages. Correct answers rose from 21% to 87% among gynecologists.
- Guideline: pair percentages with absolute numbers. A frame is hard to abuse when the raw number sits next to it.
- Guideline: keep "accept" and "decline" symmetric in tone, size and color. Asymmetry is a hidden thumb on the scale.
- Guideline: use loss framing only for real, verifiable risks. Invented urgency is a lie, and users can tell.
- Guideline: real head starts motivate (pre-stamped cards: 34% vs 19% completion). Fake progress is a lie.
- Guideline: test comprehension, not only conversion. If 2 of 5 users restate the offer wrongly, the frame misleads.
- For us: write the "why" line with an absolute number ("320 searches/month in Nürnberg"), not a bare percentage, and only use loss wording for a real ranking risk.

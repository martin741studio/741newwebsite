# What effective SEO client onboarding looks like: The first 90 days

Most SEO problems with clients don’t start with the work itself. They start with how the partnership is set up.

That’s because onboarding goes far beyond admin. Onboarding is where trust gets built, expectations are set, and ways of working are established. But more than anything else, onboarding is about understanding the organization behind the website.

A website doesn’t exist in isolation. Instead, it reflects a business with its own priorities, constraints, internal processes, and ways of making decisions. The agencies that understand this early are the ones that build strong, lasting client partnerships.

Let’s take a look at what that looks like over the first 90 days: understanding the organization you’re working with, aligning with its needs, and delivering on its priorities.

## Days 1-30: Understanding the organization, not just the website

Good onboarding begins before any audit or keyword research is conducted. The first priority is to gain a keen understanding of the business, what its commercial objectives are, and what success actually means to the client.

I know that this sounds obvious. But, in practice, it gets skipped more often than it should. Teams are keen to get into the work, and clients are keen to see the results, which can lead to teams charging ahead without actually taking the time to understand everything they need to know to be successful.

Rushing past discovery means making assumptions about what the client needs, and those assumptions have a tendency to create problems several months down the road.

The questions worth asking at the start of any engagement include:

- How does this business measure success?
- What has this business tried before, and what happened?
- Who in this business owns the website, the content, and the development resource?
- What does the client expect to see in month one, month three, and month six?

That last question matters more than most. Misaligned expectations about timelines are one of the most common sources of frustration in agency relationships, and they almost always trace back to a conversation that didn’t happen early enough.

The kickoff call is the natural starting point. Done well, it covers more than just introductions. It also works to establish the team, confirm what will be delivered in the first two months, explain how work gets communicated each month, and collect the access and information needed to start.

Everything discussed should be documented, sent to the client for confirmation, and stored somewhere both sides can access.

The first 30 days also aren’t necessarily the right time to expect meaningful movement in results. Most of the work in this period should instead be foundational.

In practice, that means making sure reporting is set up correctly, the right metrics are being tracked, and both sides agree on what success will look like before results start coming in.

### Establishing responsibilities and expectations

One of the most practical steps to take during onboarding is to establish a clear picture of responsibilities. A lot of agency and client friction comes from assumptions about who owns what. The earlier those assumptions are surfaced and agreed upon, the less friction there will be later on.

On the agency side, the SEO team owns the strategy, the recommendations, the delivery, and the communication. That includes keeping the client informed when the work is happening behind the scenes and flagging issues early rather than waiting for a reporting cycle.

On the client side, the agency team needs more from them than most clients initially realize. These include access to their analytics, GSC, the CMS, and any other necessary tools.

Business context has to come from the client, too. That means sharing product priorities, commercial goals, and the kind of information that helps the agency make better decisions that fully align with their client’s needs. It also means helping the agency understand what the business actually offers, including its products and services, as well as who its customers are. An agency can’t make smart recommendations without understanding the business it is working for, and that knowledge has to be provided, not assumed.

Implementation needs a clear owner, whether that sits with the client, their development team, or the agency itself. One person or team needs to be seen as the go-to for signing off on content and recommendations to avoid things getting lost or forgotten. Their responses to questions and requests need to happen within a reasonable timeframe.

Understanding what each side is responsible for from day one will help to prevent a large proportion of the friction that tends to surface later down the line.

### What understanding looks like in the real world

In one of our client relationships, the initial discovery work identified several technical improvements that could have a meaningful impact. Rather than immediately turning those into a delivery list, our team spent time understanding how changes were actually implemented within the client’s business.

That conversation uncovered an important constraint: The client’s development team had limited capacity and a fixed release schedule. Some recommendations could be implemented quickly, while others would need to be planned alongside larger development projects down the road.

Knowing this early allowed our team to prioritize accordingly. The first phase focused on changes that could be implemented without significant development involvement. Larger recommendations were documented and aligned with the client’s existing roadmap.

The result was a realistic plan with clear ownership and fewer surprises. The lesson to take away from our experience is that understanding the organization behind the website matters as much as understanding the website itself.

## Days 31-60: Alignment

This is where the relationship moves from setup into something more substantive. The first reporting period is a critical moment. How findings are presented to clients with varying understandings of SEO will shape how they engage with the work for months to come.

Reporting should lead with business outcomes rather than SEO metrics. For many clients, that means revenue and leads. For others, it might be visibility in a specific market or share of voice against competitors. The principle is the same: Start with what the business cares about, then use SEO metrics to explain how the work contributes to it. Rankings and traffic are useful context, but they’re not what the business is paying for.

This is also the right time to present the initial roadmap and talk through priorities. A roadmap shaped by the client’s actual capacity and constraints is far more likely to get buy-in and be implemented than an idealized list of recommendations that doesn’t account for how the business works.

One of the most common mistakes at this stage is failing to communicate what’s happening behind the scenes. Technical SEO work is largely invisible to the client. Without regular updates on what’s in progress and when the next deliverable is due, clients can easily assume that nothing is happening. A brief, consistent communication rhythm prevents that assumption from taking hold.

## Days 61-90: Momentum

The third phase is about moving from planning into execution and maintaining confidence while the work builds.

The key principle here isn’t that SEO takes a long time, but that you should never promise a particular outcome or timeframe before you understand the starting point, the implementation speed, and the market. Some technical fixes can produce visible results within 90 days. Others take longer. What matters is that the client understands this before they start, not after they’ve been waiting for three months.

This is where the early groundwork pays off. A client who understands the plan, trusts the team, and knows what to expect is far easier to work with through the slower months than one whose expectations were never properly set. Regular communication, honest reporting, and a roadmap that was agreed upon rather than imposed all contribute to a relationship that holds up under pressure.

## Start as you mean to go on

The first 90 days don’t need to produce dramatic results. They need to produce a client who understands the plan, trusts the team, and knows what’s coming next.

The agencies that get onboarding right aren’t necessarily the ones with the most sophisticated processes. They’re the ones that take the time to understand the organization behind the website, set honest expectations from the start, and make it easy for both sides to do their part.

Good onboarding doesn’t just set up the SEO work. It sets up the entire client relationship.

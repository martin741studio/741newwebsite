Phase 1Done, real data

Find proven demand before you record anything

Never guess what your audience wants. Find videos they are already choosing to watch far more than expected, and copy the demand — not the video.

What it is

You search YouTube with the words your audience would type. You do not look for the biggest videos. You look for outliers: a video that got many times more views than its own channel usually gets.

A 20,000-view video on a channel that averages 500 views is a stronger signal than a 2-million-view video on a 3-million-subscriber channel. The first one proves the topic pulled people in, not the channel's existing fame.

Why it matters

Most channels fail because the creator films what they find interesting. Outliers show what the market finds interesting, with proof already attached.

The method also looks at the losers on the same channel. Same creator, same production, but one video died — that gap tells you the exact mistake to avoid.

Phase 2Drafted, not tested

Bridge your method to a goal they already want

Nobody searches for "backend automation". Everybody searches for "scale my agency". The title has to connect the two.

What it is

Your audience knows their desired outcome (more clients, more money, fewer hours). They do not know your mechanism (Zapier flows, CRM routing, tracking setup). The bridge title puts both in one sentence so the outcome earns the click and the mechanism makes it yours.

The formula from the method: "If you don't understand [mechanism], you don't understand [outcome]."

Why it matters

The outcome gives you reach — many people want it. The mechanism gives you a reason to be different from every other "how to scale" video. Without the mechanism, you compete with everyone. Without the outcome, nobody clicks.

Drafted for you
  • "How I automated client onboarding for a design agency and got them 6 hours back a week"
  • "If you don't understand your tech stack, you don't understand why your agency can't scale past $[X]k/mo"
  • "The backend automation that let a creative agency take on 3× the clients with the same team"

All three follow the Phase 1 rule: a specific number, a stated transformation. Numbers in brackets are placeholders until you confirm a real one.

Open — needs you
  • One real number from a real client engagement (hours saved, clients added, revenue). Without it, these titles stay hypotheses.
  • Whether Tacheles can be the named example, or must stay anonymous.
Phase 3Direction set, nothing designed

Title and thumbnail: win the two-second glance

Demand exists whether you show up or not. Packaging decides how much of it you capture against the 20 other videos in the feed.

What it is

The viewer glances twice. Glance 1, the thumbnail: high-contrast text, a visible result, an authority cue (a real dashboard, a real screen). Glance 2, the title: names the exact problem and promise.

The method's test: put your thumbnail next to the real competitors in a mock feed. Does it stand out in three seconds?

Why it matters

YouTube is winner-take-all per feed slot. Two videos with the same content and different packaging can differ by 10× in views. Phase 1 already showed this: same channel, same topic, one title got 15.8× and the vague version got 8 views.

Direction for you
  • Thumbnail: the real automation flow or a real before/after dashboard on screen. Red block behind the real number. Your actual workspace, not stock.
  • Title: specific number + stated transformation. See Phase 2 drafts.
  • Avoid: listicles, "101", "secret" teases with no number, a client name with no result, year stamps. Each of these died on a real channel in your niche.
Open — needs you
  • Pick which real screen we can show (Zapier canvas, GoHighLevel, n8n, your own dashboard).
  • Then I design 2–3 thumbnail candidates and run the mock-feed test against the outlier videos above.
Phase 4Framework only, no script yet

The first 45 seconds: Promise, Proof, Plan

After the click, the question changes from "which video?" to "should I keep watching this one?" The opening answers it or loses them.

What it is

Promise (0:00–0:10): confirm in one sentence that they get what the title said.

Proof (0:10–0:25): one specific reason you can deliver it — a named result, a real number. Not "I'm an expert".

Plan (0:25–0:45): say the steps out loud, in order. The viewer commits to the whole video because they know where it goes.

Why it matters

Most drop-off happens in the first 30 seconds, and the usual cause is a broken promise: the title said one thing, the intro rambles about another. The method optimises for viewer satisfaction, not jump cuts — give them more than the title promised, remove every second that does not clarify or prove.

Applied to your video 1

Promise: "I'm going to show you the exact onboarding automation we built for a design agency, and the number it changed."

Proof: the real client + real number from Phase 2 (still open).

Plan: "Three parts: what was broken, the flow we built, and what it did to their week."

Phase 5Drafted, not built

Turn a viewer into a lead without a pitch

Views do not pay. The call to action must feel like the most useful next step of the video, not a commercial break.

What it is

Replace "book a call" with a give: a resource that extends the lesson (a template, a checklist, a scorecard). The viewer opts in to get it. The opt-in page then leads to your booking link.

The method's test: would an unbiased expert still recommend this resource? If yes, it belongs in the video. If it only exists to sell, cut it.

Why it matters

A hard pitch breaks trust and retention at once. A useful give keeps both, and it filters: only people with the actual problem download an onboarding template.

Drafted for you
  • The onboarding automation template (Zapier/GoHighLevel). It mirrors the strongest demand signal in Phase 1, so the give and the video match exactly.
  • A free agency tech-stack audit — mirrors the "agency tech stack" keyword directly.
  • Flow: video → opt-in → booking link (Calendly for EN, per your existing convention).
Open — needs you
  • Pick one give for video 1. Template is the stronger match.
  • Where the opt-in lives: your existing Supabase lead system, or a new page?
Phase 6Cannot start until a video is live

After publishing: find the leak, fix that stage only

Treat the channel as one campaign. When a video underperforms, the numbers tell you which phase failed.

The diagnostic table you will use
  • High impressions, low clicks → packaging failed → back to Phase 3 (re-do thumbnail/title).
  • Clicks, but drop-off in the first 30 s → broken promise → back to Phase 4 (re-script the opening).
  • Watched to the end, but no opt-ins → CTA felt like a pitch → back to Phase 5 (swap in a real give).
  • Opt-ins, but unqualified calls → opt-in page needs a filter question; video needs to name the avatar more precisely.

Also from the method: do not chase a one-off viral video outside your niche. Off-topic viewers ruin the channel's audience signal for every later video.

Words used above

So you can read the research files without me.

Outlier
A video that got far more views than its own channel's average. Proof that the topic, not the channel, pulled people in.
Multiplier (×)
That video's views divided by the channel's average views per video. 10× = ten times normal.
Channel average
Total channel views ÷ number of videos. We computed it live from the YouTube API for every channel checked.
Negative evidence
Videos that died on a channel that also has winners. The difference between them is the lesson.
Mechanism
Your specific method (backend automation). Unknown to the audience.
Desired outcome
What the audience already wants and searches for (scale the agency, fewer hours).
Packaging
Title + thumbnail. Decides the click.
PPP
Promise, Proof, Plan — the first 45 seconds.
Give / lead magnet
A useful resource offered instead of a pitch. The viewer opts in; the opt-in leads to your booking page.
